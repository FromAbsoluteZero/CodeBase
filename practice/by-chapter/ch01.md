## Questions that start here (9)

<a id="q40"></a>

**Q40.** When would you choose not to use machine learning?

<sub>Chapters [1](../by-chapter/ch01.md) &nbsp;·&nbsp; beginner &nbsp;·&nbsp; conceptual</sub>

<details>
<summary>What a strong answer contains</summary>

Strong answers name concrete conditions: the rules are already known and stable, data is scarce, errors are intolerable or must be fully explained, or latency and cost budgets rule out a model. The best answers add that a rules-based or statistical solution is easier to audit and maintain.

</details>

<a id="q41"></a>

**Q41.** Explain the difference between supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning.

<sub>Chapters [1](../by-chapter/ch01.md) · [37](../by-chapter/ch37.md) &nbsp;·&nbsp; beginner &nbsp;·&nbsp; conceptual</sub>

<details>
<summary>What a strong answer contains</summary>

Supervised has labeled input-output pairs and learns a predictor; unsupervised has inputs only and finds structure such as clusters or low-dimensional representations; reinforcement learning has an environment and reward signal and learns a policy through interaction. Naming one real use case for each is expected.

</details>

<a id="q42"></a>

**Q42.** What is a baseline model and why does it matter?

<sub>Chapters [1](../by-chapter/ch01.md) · [15](../by-chapter/ch15.md) &nbsp;·&nbsp; beginner &nbsp;·&nbsp; conceptual</sub>

<details>
<summary>What a strong answer contains</summary>

A trivially simple predictor — majority class, mean, or the incumbent business rule — that establishes the score a real model must beat. It quantifies the value the model actually adds and frequently reveals that a complex model adds nothing.

</details>

<a id="q168"></a>

**Q168.** A stakeholder wants to add AI to a workflow. How do you evaluate whether it is worth doing?

<sub>Chapters [1](../by-chapter/ch01.md) &nbsp;·&nbsp; intermediate &nbsp;·&nbsp; scenario</sub>

<details>
<summary>What a strong answer contains</summary>

Define the decision being made, the cost of each error, the baseline, and the data available. Ask what changes operationally if the model works, and whether a rule or a report would suffice.

</details>

<a id="q171"></a>

**Q171.** An executive asks whether you should build or buy an AI capability.

<sub>Chapters [1](../by-chapter/ch01.md) &nbsp;·&nbsp; intermediate &nbsp;·&nbsp; scenario</sub>

> **Beyond the book.** Build-versus-buy decisions are not covered in this book. Chapter 1's framing of a decision is the starting point.

<details>
<summary>What a strong answer contains</summary>

Compare on differentiation, data advantage, time to value, total cost including maintenance, and switching risk. Build where proprietary data creates an edge; buy commodity capability.

</details>

<a id="q174"></a>

**Q174.** Revenue is down 8%. How do you investigate?

<sub>Chapters [1](../by-chapter/ch01.md) · [12](../by-chapter/ch12.md) &nbsp;·&nbsp; intermediate &nbsp;·&nbsp; scenario</sub>

<details>
<summary>What a strong answer contains</summary>

Decompose before theorizing: price against volume, new against returning, by segment, region, and channel, and check whether the comparison period is fair. Strong answers rule out measurement causes — a tracking change, a pipeline failure, a calendar effect — before reaching for commercial explanations.

</details>

<a id="q183"></a>

**Q183.** You are given a vague request with no clear success metric. What do you do?

<sub>Chapters [1](../by-chapter/ch01.md) &nbsp;·&nbsp; any level &nbsp;·&nbsp; behavioural</sub>

<details>
<summary>What a strong answer contains</summary>

Work backward from the decision the output will inform, propose a measurable proxy, agree it in writing, and start with the smallest version that tests the assumption.

</details>

<a id="q185"></a>

**Q185.** Why analytics?

<sub>Chapters [1](../by-chapter/ch01.md) &nbsp;·&nbsp; any level &nbsp;·&nbsp; behavioural</sub>

<details>
<summary>What a strong answer contains</summary>

No correct answer, and the interviewer is listening for something specific rather than a general enthusiasm for data. Strong answers name a concrete thing the candidate found out, why it mattered, and what they did next.

</details>

<a id="q220"></a>

**Q220.** Summarize a year of retail.csv the way a first analysis brief would: net revenue, the cost of cancellations, the best and worst months, the leading category and how much repeat business there is.

<sub>Chapters [1](../by-chapter/ch01.md) · [4](../by-chapter/ch04.md) · [11](../by-chapter/ch11.md) · [12](../by-chapter/ch12.md) &nbsp;·&nbsp; intermediate &nbsp;·&nbsp; coding</sub>

Exercise: [challenges/q220-a-year-of-retail-in-one-function](../exercises/challenges/q220-a-year-of-retail-in-one-function/) · data: `retail.csv`

<details>
<summary>What a strong answer contains</summary>

One function returning a dictionary, built from the primitives of the earlier exercises: net revenue is a plain sum because cancellations are negative; the cancellation share is the cancelled revenue against gross; months and categories are groupbys; repeat business is the share of identified customers with more than one distinct invoice. Strong solutions state the grain before every aggregate and say which figures the missing customer IDs cannot support.

</details>
